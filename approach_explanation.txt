I'm working on a search program in a Sokoban-like puzzle game. There are only 2 differences to classic Sokoban. First, the objective: Instead of trying to put boxes on goal tiles, the win condition is getting the player on a goal tile (in as few steps as possible). The second is an additional property on blocks: the blocks have masks indicating which subset of the 4 directions they can be pushed. I'm searching for the longest possible levels in a given rectangular size.

I started with a fairly basic approach: start in a solved state (player and goal co-located, walls elsewhere). This is the only 0-step level for that goal location. Then try all possible "undo" moves: move the player in any cardinal direction that doesn't leave the grid, removing a wall as needed (since its tile is now part of the solution), and also "pulling" a wall into a block if needed (this will be pushed in the forward solution). This gives us up to 8 1-step levels: the forward solutions can have 4 directions and may or may not push a block. An undo move, if it pulls a block, OR's the block's starting mask with the direction it needs to be pushed for this undo move to make sense.

I do a backward DFS over these undo moves. However, there's a rub: undoing a move might cause a shortcut to appear. So we need to do a forward solve to rule out shortcuts before proceeding with each step of the backward search.

The 8 1 step levels already reveal a little bit of inefficiency. Identical solutions have levels that differ in a somewhat trivial way: whether a block is pushed or not. I'm generalizing these blocks with indeterminate position as what I call QuantumBlocks (QBs).

The tricky part is handling QuantumBlocks in the forward solve. Effectively, I maintain a QuantumBlock as a mapping from start position -> current inhabited position given the current sequence of steps made by the player. Sometimes, the current inhabited position is invalid because it overlaps with a wall or non-Quantum block. In that case, the starting position is not valid for the current sequence of moves in the forward solve (though it may be for other sequences of moves). Additionally, different QuantumBlocks may accumulate into the same current position. In that case, the starting positions corresponding to that current position are invalidated jointly: either one alone is fine if the other QuantumBlock is elsewhere, but they can't both be there.

Therefore, each sequence of forward steps implies some constraints on the QuantumBlock starting positions, which may or may not be satisfiable. If we reach a shortcut, that means any valid assignment for that sequence of forward steps has a shortcut. Once we've done a full forward search, we have a (possibly empty) collection of shortcuts. Any assignment which is valid for at least one of these has a shortcut; any assignment which is valid for none of them has no shortcut. We take the QB-wise union of all non-shortcut assignments; this is the valid set of combinations to continue with the backward search. (It's actually an overapproximation because some combinations of that QB-wise union may actually have shortcuts, but the overapproximation is probably better than bifurcating the backward search.)
